Dale Mellorʼs Blog is rather good

Back at the start of the month, I got a bit opinionated and wrote “Mistakes to avoid with your personal website“. One commenter invited me to take a look at their blog. That blog was Dale Mellorʼs Blog. So, I took a look and liked what I saw.

How did Dale Mellor’s blog measure up?

Going by my list of irritations, this blog passed the first test. It answered the question “WTF is this?” Clearly, this is Dale Mellor’s personal blog with opinions on all my favourate geeky topics – science, programming, sci-fi, maths and the planet. So it already passes the first test.

The first post was, “Re: Replies Instead of Comments“, which I hoped talked about WebMention and/or ActivityPub. To my delight, it talked about both. I’m a massive nerd for anything WebMention-related. I am with Dale 100% on more things supporting WebMention. WebMention is brill, and I love it.

The typography looked a bit homemade, which I am 100% okay with. Everything was readable even for a grumpy old git like me. For those bits that were a bit small, browsers have a zoom function (Ctrl + scroll wheel). Dale’s layout knew enough to rescale and organise itself nicely when I over-zoomed. So, at all scales, the page was readable. Lovely.

I saw no unmarked AI on Dale’s blog, which makes me a happy visitor. I’ve no problem with people doing goofy things with AI; just mark it as such.

No fails found.

Something I loved

I checked out a few more posts until I found “I Discreetly Weaponized My Blog to Motivate My Writing“. My cold, dead heart was filled with glee. Not only had Dale made a gloriously extra thing, but he made it so subtle I missed it entirely. I love that. In case you were wondering what, a subtle visual divider line that is also a writing progress bar. Dale, sir, I am impressed.

I love a high-effort, totally extra thing that maybe two or three people will even see. That commitment to making strange things for the love of the game is exactly what I want to see.

I’m no stranger to making weird things and putting them online. Some, like Open Mentions, are designed to be played with and/or imitated. While other stuff is just silly nonsense. I love seeing odd things people make. In this world of AI, that’s even more important.

I don’t care if it is perfect; I care that you tried.

In praise of longer-form content

Another post that made me smile was “Polish Your Postings, Please” – an ode to making an effort with blog posts. It felt like it was in a similar spirit to my “Mistakes to avoid with your personal website“, only kinder and more inviting. Unlike Dale, I do like short thought’s posts, but I love long, thought-out posts. That’s why I run an entirely separate blog for more social-media-grade shorts. This one is for discussions; that one is for chatting.

But I totally get it. A considered article-length post is a commitment. It says that you care enough about this to write more than a paragraph. More so if you use subheadings too. I love a good subheading.

A robust defence of IndieWeb ideas

Dale put forth a well-written and robust argument in favour of IndieWeb in “The Indieweb Has Not Become Irrelevant“. The call to action for us all to “embrace the (slow) revolution” resonated strongly with me. Not only because very little that I do takes place at speed, but also because little-by-little is a good way to win at life.

Dale is right; the IndieWeb is very much alive and slowly growing.

Some honourable mentions to end with

  • The Death of Honour: Silos Must Not Be Used
  • Invitation to Join a Posse of Webmention Scratch-Builders to Work Towards Interoperability
  • My Rationale for a Dynamically-Produced Blog Site

TL;DR:

In a much-delayed answer to the comment: Dale Mellorʼs Blog is fantastic. I think you should go and give it a read and consider bookmarking it for later, too.

If you are thinking of running your own blog and would like an example to aim for, Dale’s blog is that.

#blogging #DaleMellor #IndieWeb #Webmention

A normal thing for a beginning Idris programmer is to use dependent types absolutely everywhere, to attempt to explain everything in a type. Beginning Idris programmers should definitely go through this phase, if only to learn why it's probably better not to do that.

The short answer for why to not do that is: you have merely moved the problem from the program to the type, and you've made the program harder to write in the process. The point of types, to me, is not to prove everything. Rather, it's to help you understand the problem better, and to help you come up with explanations of the data you're working with that will make the rest of your project easier to handle.
